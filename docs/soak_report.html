Voice agent: 1000 connected calls

Does the wait for an answer change when 1000 calls are connected? Test results, 4 November 2026.

In short
  • With 1000 calls connected at once on one Apple M3 Pro laptop, none was refused or dropped, and the wait for an answer barely changed: the median went from 0.48 s to 0.52 s.
  • So the latency did not drop and did not get worse in any way that matters: it stayed the same, as it should when the system is healthy.
  • This shows the router, the call-handling workers and the database cope with 1000 connections. It does not show 1000 people talking at once. That needs rented machines (last section).

What was tested

The real router, 4 real call-handling workers, a real PostgreSQL database and the real model (Nemotron on Amazon Bedrock), all on one laptop. The speech recogniser and the voice were replaced by fast stand-ins, so this test measures everything except those two services (they were measured separately, 3 November).

  1. Baseline: 4 real conversations (computer voices speaking to the server through its WebSocket, microphone open, five different conversations) with nothing else connected.
  2. Ramp: 1000 more callers connect over 150 seconds. Each asks the router for a worker, opens the line, hears the greeting, and stays on the line sending a short burst of silence every second.
  3. Hold: with all 1000 connected, the same 4 real conversations run again.

What this does not show

  • The callers were quiet. They sent one short burst of silence a second. A real browser microphone sends 50 a second, which costs the worker far more. With open microphones the same laptop managed 200 callers (about 2.4% of a core each), and at 400 the wait for 4 real conversations rose from 0.45 s to 7.2 s, because one worker process cannot hold about 100 calls at once. Production allows 16 calls per worker for this reason.
  • The recogniser and voice were stand-ins. The real ones handle one request at a time and ran out at 12 to 16 talking callers on this laptop (report of 3 November).
  • One laptop, not a deployment. Real traffic across 60 or more workers, a managed database and real networks has not been tested.

Latency stays about the same from 100 to 1000 callers only when the hardware grows with the callers. With half the hardware it collapses.

What 1000 to 2000 talking callers needs

  • Call-handling workers: about 63 to 72 processes at 16 calls each, roughly 180 processor cores for 1000 callers (twice that for 2000).
  • Voice servers: about 80 copies, roughly 160 cores, for 1000 callers.
  • Speech recognition: a GPU server that batches requests, or 15 to 45 copies of the recognisers measured here.
  • The model on Bedrock: about 100 requests a second at 1000 callers.
  • Separate machines to generate the callers, and a managed database and router.

Why not this laptop: it has 12 processor cores and its voice and recogniser servers handle one request at a time. The voice is slower on the laptop's GPU than on its processor (4.4 against 7.8 seconds of speech per second of work), and the workers are ordinary program code that a GPU does not run. Rough rental cost of a 4 to 6 hour test: about $100 to $200 for machines at 1000 callers, plus the model's token charges, which are the main unknown.
